Why Wall Street Sold Off: AI Stocks, Data Center Spending and What Investors Are Watching

US stocks fell sharply as investors reassessed AI and data-center valuations after the 2026 midterms. Here is what happened to the S&P 500, Nasdaq, Dow, Treasury yields and the next inflation data.

November 6, 2026 · Rajasthan News 24 US Finance Desk

What happened in the stock market

Wall Street suffered a sharp sell-off on Thursday, November 5, 2026, led by companies tied to artificial-intelligence infrastructure and data-center construction. The Associated Press reported that the S&P 500 fell 4.4% to 7,353.73, the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 1,833 points, or 3.4%, to 51,523.06, and the Nasdaq Composite declined 5.5% to 24,315. The small-cap Russell 2000 fell 4.4%. The declines erased the previous session's gains and marked the steepest broad-market setback since April 2025, according to AP's market recap.

Why AI and data-center shares came under pressure

Investors have spent years pricing in rapid growth from generative AI, cloud computing and the infrastructure needed to train and run advanced models. That investment requires data centers, specialized chips, networking equipment, power generation, cooling systems and long-term financing. The market's concern is not that AI has suddenly stopped being useful. It is whether the expected revenue and productivity gains will arrive quickly enough to justify the enormous capital spending and high valuations attached to some companies. The post-election political environment added another layer of uncertainty. Reports of local measures opposing data-center development and expectations of tougher scrutiny in some states raised questions about permitting, electricity demand, water use and construction timelines. Investors are trying to estimate whether new rules could delay projects or increase costs. These are risks being priced by markets, not proof that every proposed data center will be cancelled.

How the major indexes performed

The S&P 500 is a broad benchmark of large US companies. The Dow tracks 30 major companies and is price-weighted, while the Nasdaq Composite has a heavier concentration of technology and growth-oriented stocks. Because the Nasdaq has greater exposure to technology companies, it can fall more sharply when investors reduce risk in high-valuation technology shares. AP reported that the S&P 500 was still up about 7.4% for 2026 after Thursday's decline, while the Dow remained up about 7.2%, the Nasdaq 4.6% and the Russell 2000 10% for the year. Those year-to-date figures show why a single severe session does not automatically mean the market has entered a prolonged bear market. It does, however, show how quickly sentiment can change when investors question earnings expectations, financing costs or future regulation.

What happened to individual technology stocks

The sell-off was especially severe in semiconductor and AI-related shares. AP reported that Nvidia fell about 9.3%, while Advanced Micro Devices dropped roughly 17.3%. Data-center power provider GE Vernova also declined. These moves reflect the sensitivity of companies across the AI supply chain: chipmakers depend on customer spending plans, equipment suppliers depend on construction schedules, and utilities or power companies depend on long-term demand forecasts. Not every company moved lower. AP highlighted sharp gains in AppLovin after strong results and a larger share buyback plan, and a rise in Tapestry after the company raised its outlook. That contrast is important: a broad market sell-off does not mean every business has the same earnings outlook. Company-specific revenue, margins, guidance, debt and cash flow still matter.

What investors are watching next: inflation and the Federal Reserve

The next major US data point is the October Consumer Price Index report, scheduled for November 10. Inflation data can influence expectations for Federal Reserve policy because persistent price increases may make policymakers cautious about cutting rates or could strengthen the case for tighter policy. Investors will also study employment, consumer spending and company earnings to judge whether the economy is slowing or remaining resilient. Interest-rate expectations affect stock valuations in several ways. Higher Treasury yields can make future corporate profits less valuable in today's dollars, increase borrowing costs and make bonds more competitive with equities. Technology companies whose valuations rely heavily on expected future growth can be particularly sensitive to changes in discount rates. But a single inflation report rarely determines the Fed's next move by itself. Policymakers review a wider set of data, including inflation trends, labour-market conditions and financial stability.

Why Treasury bonds and oil matter to the story

During periods of market stress, some investors move money toward US Treasury securities, which are widely treated as a benchmark safe asset. Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions: when demand for existing bonds rises, their prices generally rise and yields fall. A decline in Treasury yields can ease some financing pressure, although it can also signal that investors expect weaker growth or are seeking protection. Oil prices can complicate the inflation outlook. Higher energy costs can feed into transportation, manufacturing and household budgets. Reuters noted that markets were also focused on oil-price risks and upcoming inflation data as investors reassessed the outlook for rates. Energy prices are influenced by supply, demand, inventories, geopolitical events and currency movements, so daily price swings should not be treated as a reliable forecast of future inflation.

What this could mean for everyday Americans

A stock-market decline does not directly change every household's paycheque or bank balance. Its effect depends on whether a person owns investments, has retirement savings linked to stocks, works in an affected industry or is planning a large purchase. People with 401(k) plans and IRAs may see account balances fluctuate because many retirement funds hold stock and bond investments. A decline in account value is not the same as a realised loss unless investments are sold, but it can matter for someone close to retirement or making withdrawals. Market volatility can also influence business confidence, hiring plans and the cost of raising capital. If companies delay data-center projects, suppliers and construction firms could face slower orders. If AI investment continues despite the sell-off, the impact may be temporary repricing rather than a lasting slowdown. Consumers should watch employment, wage growth, credit conditions and prices rather than relying on the stock index alone to judge the economy.

What long-term investors should avoid doing

A sharp market move can create pressure to sell immediately or chase whichever asset is rising. Neither reaction is a substitute for a plan. Investors should revisit their time horizon, emergency savings, debt costs, diversification and ability to tolerate losses. A retirement portfolio designed for decades should not necessarily be changed because of one trading session. On the other hand, a portfolio that is concentrated in a few expensive stocks may deserve a careful risk review. Avoid making decisions from social-media predictions, anonymous trading tips or claims that a crash is guaranteed. No analyst can know with certainty whether a sharp decline will be followed by a quick rebound or a longer downturn. Consider fees, taxes and the consequences of selling before making changes. If the decision involves retirement income, taxes or a large share of household wealth, a qualified fiduciary financial adviser may help assess the full situation.

What to watch over the coming days

Investors will be looking for the October CPI release, further comments from Federal Reserve officials, corporate earnings and updates on AI infrastructure spending. They will also watch Treasury yields, oil prices and the market's response to new information about regulation and data-center permits. The most useful signal is likely to come from a combination of earnings guidance, actual spending plans and economic data rather than a single headline. For official US economic data, the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes inflation and employment reports, the Federal Reserve publishes monetary-policy statements and economic releases, and the US Treasury publishes information about government securities. Company filings and investor-relations releases provide primary information about revenue, capital spending, debt and guidance.
