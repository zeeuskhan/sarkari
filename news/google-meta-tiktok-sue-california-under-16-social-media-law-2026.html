Google, Meta and TikTok Sue to Block California’s Under-16 Social Media Law

Published November 7, 2026 · Rajasthan News 24 Desk

Google, Meta and TikTok have filed separate lawsuits challenging California rules on addictive social media features for users under 16. The law is scheduled to take effect January 1, 2027.

SAN FRANCISCO, November 7, 2026: Google, Meta and TikTok have filed separate federal lawsuits seeking to stop California from enforcing a new law that restricts certain social media features for children under 16. The companies argue that the measure violates constitutional protections for speech and improperly limits how platforms organize and recommend content.

The law is scheduled to take effect on January 1, 2027, unless a court blocks it or changes its implementation. The lawsuits place California’s effort to regulate youth social media use at the centre of a growing national debate over child safety, parental authority, privacy and the rights of technology companies.

What does California’s law seek to restrict?

The measure targets features that state lawmakers describe as addictive or psychologically exploitative when offered to users under 16. The Associated Press reports that the law covers features such as endless scrolling, autoplayed videos and personalized feeds. It also requires platforms to take reasonable steps to verify age and prevent underage users from receiving the restricted features.

The policy is aimed at the design of online services, not at banning every form of internet access for teenagers. A young person could still use digital services that do not provide the restricted features, subject to the law’s definitions and any later court rulings. The exact scope will depend on how the statute is interpreted and enforced.

Why are Google, Meta and TikTok suing?

The companies filed separate cases in federal courts in San Francisco, Los Angeles and San Jose. Their central argument is that the state is regulating how platforms present information and select content for users. They say those decisions involve editorial judgment protected by the First Amendment.

The companies have also raised arguments under Section 230 of the federal Communications Decency Act, a law that provides certain protections to online services for material posted by users. The legal questions are not identical in every case, and a court will need to examine the text of the California law, the companies’ claims and the state’s response before deciding whether any part of the measure can be enforced.

Google has argued that broad restrictions could interfere with systems designed to present age-appropriate material. Meta says the state should not dictate how ideas are organized and displayed. TikTok argues that content curation is a form of protected expression. These are the companies’ positions in the litigation, not findings that a court has accepted.

How has California responded?

The office of California Attorney General Rob Bonta said it was reviewing the lawsuits and intended to defend the state’s child-protection laws, according to the Associated Press. Governor Gavin Newsom signed the measure in September. State officials have presented the law as an effort to reduce risks associated with platform design and compulsive use by young people.

Supporters argue that features such as autoplay and endless feeds can make it harder for children to stop using an app and may encourage prolonged engagement. Critics, including digital-rights advocates, have raised concerns about age verification, privacy and the possibility that broad rules could limit access to useful information or online communities.

Will the law change social media use immediately?

Not on the basis of the lawsuits alone. The law has a scheduled effective date of January 1, 2027. Filing a case does not automatically suspend a statute. Unless a judge issues an order blocking or delaying enforcement, the law remains on the books and the state can continue preparing to implement it.

That means families should be careful with viral posts claiming that Instagram, Facebook, YouTube or TikTok are already being shut down for every California teenager. The legal dispute concerns specific features and age-based rules. It is not a statewide shutdown of social media, and the courts have not yet issued a final decision on the companies’ constitutional arguments.

What could the court decide?

A judge could deny the companies’ requests and allow the law to move toward its January 1 start date. A court could also issue a temporary order while it considers the case, block particular provisions or eventually rule that some parts of the statute cannot be enforced. Any decision may be appealed, which could extend the dispute beyond the start of 2027.

The key issues include how the law defines covered platforms and restricted features, whether age checks are sufficiently clear, how the requirements affect protected expression and whether the state has chosen a legally permissible way to pursue its child-safety goals. Courts will weigh those questions against the record and arguments presented by both sides.

What parents and teenagers should know

Parents do not need to delete every social media app because of the lawsuits. They can review each service’s existing family and teen settings, discuss screen-time expectations and check official updates from California before making decisions based on a headline. Teenagers should understand that the legal process is still underway and that the rules may be clarified by court orders.

For families outside California, the case is still worth watching. Other states are considering how to regulate youth accounts, recommendation systems and age verification. A ruling on the First Amendment or Section 230 arguments could influence how similar laws are drafted elsewhere, although it would not automatically decide every state’s policy.

What happens next?

The next major developments will be court filings, any requests for emergency relief and the state’s formal responses. Until a judge issues an order, the January 1, 2027 effective date remains the schedule stated in the law. The litigation is unresolved, and neither the companies nor California has won a final ruling.

The bottom line: The lawsuits are an important test of how far states can go in regulating the design of social media products used by children. California says it is protecting minors; the companies say the law interferes with speech and editorial choices. The courts, not the initial filings, will determine which arguments prevail.
