U.S. Employers Add 165,000 Jobs in October as Unemployment Falls to 4.1%

Published November 6, 2026 · 2:04 p.m. ET · Rajasthan News 24 US Desk

U.S. payrolls increased by 165,000 in October, while the unemployment rate eased to 4.1%. Wage growth remained moderate, and several measures show that job-market strain has not disappeared.

WASHINGTON, November 6, 2026: U.S. payrolls increased by 165,000 in October, while the unemployment rate eased to 4.1%. Wage growth remained moderate, and several measures show that job-market strain has not disappeared.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that nonfarm payroll employment rose by 165,000 in October. September’s gain was revised up to 60,000, and August was revised to 186,000. The two revisions added a combined 84,000 jobs to previously reported totals.

What the report showed

The unemployment rate fell to 4.1% from 4.2% in September. The separate household survey counted 463,000 more people as employed and 421,000 more people in the labor force. The labor-force participation rate edged up to 61.9%. These household figures are not the same measure as the 165,000 payroll gain, which comes from an employer survey.

Average hourly earnings reached $38.00, up 14 cents over the month, or about 0.4%. Reuters reported annual wage growth of 3.1%. The average workweek held at 34.4 hours.

Which industries added jobs?

Professional and business services added 70,000 positions. Health care and social assistance added 55,100, transportation and warehousing added 24,800, and manufacturing added 19,000. Leisure and hospitality employment fell by 13,000, while construction lost 3,000 jobs.

Why the headline is not the whole story

The household survey showed 1.963 million people had been unemployed for at least 27 weeks. The number of people working part time for economic reasons rose by 339,000 to 4.84 million. Those measures point to continuing difficulty for some job seekers even as the headline unemployment rate improved.

The three-month average payroll gain was 137,000. Monthly estimates can be revised as more employer data arrive, and the two BLS surveys can move differently.

What it could mean for the Fed

A stronger labor market can give the Federal Reserve more room to keep rates elevated if inflation remains above its goal. It can also support household income and spending. One jobs report does not decide policy: officials will weigh inflation, wages, hiring and financial conditions before the next scheduled FOMC meeting on December 8–9.
