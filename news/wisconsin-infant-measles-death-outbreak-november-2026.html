Wisconsin Reports First Infant Measles Death Since 1990 as Outbreak Reaches 431 Cases

MADISON, WISCONSIN, November 6, 2026: The Wisconsin Department of Health Services said an infant died in connection with the measles outbreak in southwestern Wisconsin. The state had recorded 431 cases in 13 counties and nine hospitalizations as of November 5.

The Wisconsin Department of Health Services said an infant has died in connection with the ongoing measles outbreak in southwestern Wisconsin. The coroner listed measles as the immediate cause of death, the department said in a November 5 release. It is the first measles-related death reported in Wisconsin since 1990.

State officials withheld the child’s age, location and other personal details to protect the family’s privacy.

Wisconsin outbreak reaches 431 cases

As of November 5, Wisconsin had confirmed 431 measles cases across 13 counties, according to the state health department. Nine people had been hospitalized with severe symptoms. The state said the total was the highest number of cases seen in Wisconsin in more than 35 years.

The largest cluster has been in southwestern Wisconsin, including Grant, Iowa, Lafayette and Vernon counties. Confirmed counts may understate the true spread because not everyone who becomes ill seeks medical care or gets tested.

Who faces a higher risk?

Measles can cause serious complications, including pneumonia and swelling of the brain. Children younger than 5, pregnant people and people with weakened immune systems are at increased risk. The disease spreads through the air and can remain infectious in a space after an infected person has left.

Wisconsin DHS says two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine are about 97% effective at preventing measles. The department said it had delivered 2,000 MMR doses to local health departments in affected communities since August 1, and providers had administered more than 42,000 doses statewide during that period.

What residents should do

Residents can check vaccination records through the Wisconsin Immunization Registry or contact a health care provider or local health department. Anyone who believes they have been exposed should follow public-health instructions and call ahead before visiting a clinic if symptoms develop.
