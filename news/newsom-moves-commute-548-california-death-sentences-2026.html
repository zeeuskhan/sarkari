Newsom Moves to Commute 548 California Death Sentences; Court Review Needed in 382 Cases

Published November 7, 2026 · Rajasthan News 24 Desk

California Gov. Gavin Newsom says he plans to change 548 death sentences to life without parole. The state Supreme Court must approve 382 of the proposed commutations.

SACRAMENTO, November 7, 2026: California Gov. Gavin Newsom says he plans to change all 548 death sentences in the state to life imprisonment without the possibility of parole. The proposal would be one of the largest clemency actions in U.S. history, but it is not complete: the California Supreme Court must approve 382 of the proposed commutations because those prisoners have multiple felony convictions.

The announcement has reopened a long-running debate over capital punishment, victims’ rights, wrongful convictions and the power of a governor to grant clemency. Newsom’s office says the plan would apply to 529 men and 19 women currently sentenced to death in California.

What Newsom announced

Newsom released a public letter on November 5 saying he intends to change the sentences to life without parole. In the letter, he argued that the death penalty conflicts with the values he believes California should uphold and that the state’s justice system should not carry out executions.

The governor’s announcement is a decision to pursue commutations, not a declaration that the prisoners have been released. A commutation changes a sentence. In this case, the proposed replacement is life imprisonment without the possibility of parole, meaning the sentence would not provide a regular parole route to release.

Why the California Supreme Court must review many cases

According to information provided by the governor’s office to the Associated Press, 382 of the 548 cases require approval from the California Supreme Court. The state constitution limits a governor’s ability to grant clemency to people with multiple felony convictions. The court review is therefore a legal step, not simply an administrative formality.

The remaining cases do not all follow the same approval path. The important point is that the announcement should not be described as an already completed commutation of every sentence. Until the required legal steps are finished, the status of individual sentences remains subject to the process that applies to each prisoner.

What life without parole means

Life without parole is a prison sentence that does not include eligibility for release through the ordinary parole process. If the proposed changes are approved, the prisoners would no longer face execution under those sentences, but they would remain incarcerated for life unless a separate legal action changed their status.

This distinction is important because some social media posts may describe the plan as freeing people from prison. That is not what Newsom announced. The proposal changes the punishment from death to life without parole. It does not erase convictions, vacate verdicts or automatically release anyone.

California’s history with the death penalty

California has one of the largest death rows in the United States. Voters upheld the state’s death penalty in ballot measures in 2012 and 2016. The state has not carried out an execution since 2006, and Newsom imposed a moratorium on executions in 2019. The moratorium halted executions while he was governor, but it did not itself erase death sentences.

The current proposal goes further than a moratorium because it seeks to change the sentences themselves. The Death Penalty Information Center told the Associated Press that, if all the commutations are approved, the action would reduce the U.S. death-row population by nearly 30 percent. That estimate depends on the full set of proposed changes taking effect.

Why the decision is drawing sharply different reactions

Supporters of the move argue that capital punishment carries a risk of irreversible error and that its use has been affected by racial and economic disparities. The American Civil Liberties Union welcomed the announcement and said the death penalty has been applied unfairly. Advocates for criminal-justice reform also argue that a sentence of life without parole protects public safety without carrying out executions.

Victims’ families and prosecutors have raised the opposite concern. Some say changing sentences disregards jury verdicts and the suffering of people who lost relatives to murder. Others argue that victims’ families should have a meaningful voice in decisions that alter punishment. The Associated Press reported strong criticism from California victims’ advocates and the state district attorneys’ association.

Those reactions reflect different views about the purpose of punishment and the authority of elected officials. They should not be reduced to a simple claim that all victims’ families support or oppose the plan. Individual families have different experiences and positions.

What happens next?

The next step is the legal review required for the 382 cases. The governor’s office and the courts will need to complete the process before those commutations can take effect. The state may also provide additional information about how the remaining cases will be handled and when the review will be completed.

Because the proposal affects hundreds of individual sentences, it is important to follow official notices rather than assume every case will be resolved on the same day. The courts’ decisions and any further clemency documents will determine the final status of each sentence.

What the announcement does and does not mean

It means the governor has announced a plan to replace 548 death sentences with life without parole. It means the state Supreme Court has a required role in 382 of those cases. It does not mean all 548 changes have already been approved, that prisoners are being released, or that California voters have repealed the death penalty through a new ballot measure.

The bottom line: Newsom’s proposal is a major development in California’s death-penalty debate, but the legal process is still underway. The most accurate way to describe it is a planned mass commutation, with court approval required in hundreds of cases, rather than a completed change to every sentence.
