Medicare in 2026: Parts A, B, C and D, Enrollment Periods, Costs and Coverage Explained

A US-focused Medicare guide explaining Original Medicare, Medicare Advantage, Part D, enrollment windows, premiums, deductibles, networks and how to compare coverage in 2026.

Updated November 6, 2026 · Rajasthan News 24 US Desk

What Medicare is and who it serves

Medicare is the federal health insurance program primarily for people age 65 and older. It also covers certain younger people with qualifying disabilities and people with End-Stage Renal Disease or ALS under federal eligibility rules. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) administers the program, while Social Security often handles enrollment for people approaching age 65. Medicare is not the same as Medicaid: Medicaid is a joint federal-state program with eligibility rules that generally focus on income and other state-defined criteria. Medicare coverage is not automatically free. Some people qualify for premium-free Part A because of their own or a spouse's Medicare-tax work history, but Part B normally has a monthly premium. Deductibles, coinsurance, copayments, prescription drug costs and coverage gaps can also apply. The total cost depends on the coverage path, income, services used, provider choices and supplemental coverage. The most important planning step is to understand the difference between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage before enrolling. Each option has trade-offs involving provider access, networks, out-of-pocket costs, extra benefits and how prescription drugs are covered.

The four main parts of Medicare

Part A is hospital insurance. It generally helps cover inpatient hospital care, qualifying skilled nursing facility care after a qualifying hospital stay, hospice care and some home health services. Part A has benefit-period rules and cost sharing, so it should not be described as unlimited free hospital coverage. Whether a person owes a Part A premium depends largely on their Medicare-tax work record or a qualifying spouse's record. Part B is medical insurance. It generally covers medically necessary physician services, outpatient care, preventive services, durable medical equipment and certain other services. Most beneficiaries pay a monthly Part B premium, and the amount can be higher for people with income above specified thresholds. Part B also has an annual deductible and commonly involves coinsurance after the deductible. Part C is Medicare Advantage. These are private plans approved by Medicare that provide the Part A and Part B benefits. Most plans include prescription drug coverage, and many offer additional benefits. Plans may use provider networks, service areas, prior authorization and plan-specific cost-sharing rules. A Medicare Advantage plan is not a Medicare supplement policy. Part D is prescription drug coverage. It is offered through private plans approved by Medicare, either as a stand-alone drug plan used with Original Medicare or as part of many Medicare Advantage plans. Formularies, pharmacy networks, premiums and drug costs differ by plan. A medicine covered by one plan may be placed on a different tier or subject to different rules in another plan.

Original Medicare versus Medicare Advantage

Original Medicare generally consists of Part A and Part B. Beneficiaries can usually see any provider nationwide who accepts Medicare, which can be useful for people who travel or want broad provider choice. Original Medicare does not include most routine outpatient prescription drug coverage, so many people add a separate Part D plan. There is no annual out-of-pocket maximum for Original Medicare by itself, which is why some beneficiaries consider a Medigap policy, also called Medicare Supplement Insurance, if they are eligible and can obtain one under applicable rules. Medicare Advantage combines Part A and Part B coverage through a private plan. Plans usually have networks and may require referrals or prior authorization for some services. They must cover Medicare-covered services, subject to program rules, but they can structure benefits and cost sharing differently. Many plans include prescription drugs and extra benefits such as certain dental, vision or hearing services, though the details and limits vary. Neither option is automatically best for everyone. Compare doctors, hospitals, prescriptions, travel patterns, premiums, deductibles, copays and the plan's maximum out-of-pocket amount. A plan with a low monthly premium may still cost more if a person uses services frequently or goes outside its network. Read the Evidence of Coverage and Annual Notice of Change rather than relying only on an advertisement.

When to enroll in Medicare

The Initial Enrollment Period generally lasts seven months: it begins three months before the month a person turns 65, includes the birthday month and continues for three months afterward. People who already receive Social Security or Railroad Retirement benefits may be enrolled automatically in some parts of Medicare. Others need to sign up. The exact steps depend on whether the person is still working, has employer coverage and qualifies for a Special Enrollment Period. A person who has health coverage through current employment, either their own or a spouse's, may be able to delay Part B without a late-enrollment penalty if the coverage meets Medicare's requirements. Retiree coverage and COBRA are not always treated like coverage from current employment for this purpose. Before declining Part B, confirm the employer size and plan rules with the employer benefits office and Medicare. A mistaken assumption can lead to a coverage gap or penalty. The General Enrollment Period and Special Enrollment Period rules can help people who missed an earlier opportunity, but they are not interchangeable. Some enrollment windows apply only to particular circumstances. Keep proof of employer coverage and dates, and submit required forms promptly. Do not wait until a medical appointment to discover that enrollment was delayed incorrectly.

Annual enrollment and changing plans

The Medicare Open Enrollment Period runs each year from October 15 through December 7. During this period, people with Medicare can generally switch between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage, change Medicare Advantage plans, join or change a Part D plan, or drop drug coverage, with changes generally effective January 1. There are other enrollment periods for specific circumstances, including the Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period from January 1 through March 31 for people already enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan. Annual plan review matters because premiums, provider networks, formularies, drug tiers, prior authorization rules and extra benefits can change from year to year. A medication that was affordable in one year may cost more in the next plan. A doctor or hospital may leave a network. Read the plan's Annual Notice of Change and compare the next year's coverage before the deadline. Do not assume that staying in the same plan is always the simplest or cheapest option. At the same time, do not switch based only on a mailer promising free benefits. Verify that your doctors, hospitals and prescriptions are covered and check the plan's total cost exposure.

What Medicare costs can include

Medicare costs can include monthly premiums, annual deductibles, coinsurance, copayments and prescription drug expenses. Part A may be premium-free for many people with sufficient work history, but inpatient hospital stays can still involve a deductible and additional cost-sharing rules. Part B generally has a monthly premium and an annual deductible, followed by coinsurance for many covered services. Higher-income beneficiaries may pay an income-related monthly adjustment amount, known as IRMAA, for Part B and Part D. Medicare Advantage plans set their own approved premiums and cost-sharing structures within federal rules. Some advertise a $0 additional plan premium, but that does not mean the beneficiary pays nothing for healthcare. The person generally must continue paying the Part B premium and may owe copays, coinsurance or other costs when receiving care. Review the annual maximum out-of-pocket limit for covered Part A and Part B services. Medigap premiums vary by insurer, plan type, location, age rating method and other factors. Medigap generally helps pay certain costs under Original Medicare but does not work with Medicare Advantage. A separate Part D plan may be needed. Because plan prices and annual figures change, use Medicare.gov's current plan finder and official cost pages for the applicable year instead of relying on a prior-year article.

Prescription drug coverage and formularies

Part D plans use formularies, which are lists of covered prescription drugs. A plan may place medicines into tiers with different cost-sharing amounts. Some prescriptions require prior authorization, step therapy or quantity limits. A pharmacy may be preferred, standard or outside the plan's network, changing the amount a member pays. Before selecting a drug plan, list every prescription, dosage and frequency. Check the exact drug name, generic alternatives, dosage form and pharmacy. Compare estimated annual costs, not just monthly premiums. A plan with a lower premium may have higher costs for a particular medicine. Ask the prescribing clinician whether an appropriate alternative exists if a medicine is not covered, but never stop or change a prescription solely because of a plan comparison. Drug coverage can be part of a Medicare Advantage plan or purchased as a stand-alone Part D plan with Original Medicare. People should understand how adding or dropping coverage affects their current arrangement. Late enrollment penalties may apply in some circumstances when a person goes without creditable prescription drug coverage for too long.

Medigap and supplemental coverage

Medigap policies are sold by private insurers to help pay certain out-of-pocket costs under Original Medicare. Standardized plan letters generally provide the same core benefits within a state, although premiums and availability vary. Massachusetts, Minnesota and Wisconsin use different standardization systems. Medigap does not pay for everything, and it generally does not cover long-term custodial care, routine dental care, vision, hearing aids or prescription drugs. The federal Medigap Open Enrollment Period is a one-time six-month period that starts when a person is 65 or older and enrolled in Part B. During this period, insurers generally cannot use medical underwriting to deny coverage or charge more because of health problems, subject to applicable law. Outside protected enrollment rights, an insurer may be able to ask health questions or deny an application depending on state law and circumstances. A person enrolled in Medicare Advantage generally cannot use a Medigap policy to pay the plan's copays and deductibles. Someone considering a move from Medicare Advantage back to Original Medicare should first understand whether they can obtain a Medigap policy and what it will cost. State insurance departments and State Health Insurance Assistance Programs (SHIPs) can help explain local rules.

Medicare and employer or retiree health insurance

People who work beyond age 65 should coordinate Medicare with employer coverage. Whether the employer plan pays first or Medicare pays first can depend on employer size, the type of coverage and the person's eligibility. For active employment coverage, large-group and small-group rules can differ. A spouse's current-employment plan may also be relevant. Retiree coverage and COBRA generally do not count as current-employment coverage for delaying Part B without consequences. Before making a decision, ask the employer benefits administrator for written information about whether the plan is creditable for prescription drugs and whether it is primary or secondary to Medicare. Ask when coverage ends if employment stops. Keep the documents because they may be needed to prove eligibility for a Special Enrollment Period. If a person has Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions through work, Medicare enrollment can affect the ability to contribute. People approaching Medicare should discuss timing with their employer and tax adviser before starting or delaying enrollment. Medicare rules and tax rules interact, so a casual assumption can be costly.

Medicare Savings Programs and Extra Help

People with limited income and resources may qualify for assistance with Medicare costs. Medicare Savings Programs are administered by states and can help pay Part A and/or Part B premiums and, depending on the category, other Medicare cost sharing. Eligibility thresholds and application processes can vary by state and are updated periodically. Extra Help is a federal program that helps eligible people with Medicare pay Part D prescription drug costs. It can reduce premiums, deductibles and copayments for covered drugs. Some people qualify automatically through other assistance programs, while others need to apply. People should not assume they are ineligible without checking current rules, because income and resource limits and pathways to qualification can change. Medicaid may also help some people who qualify for both Medicare and Medicaid. These beneficiaries are often called dual eligible. The exact benefits and coordination depend on state programs and individual eligibility. Contact the state Medicaid agency or a local SHIP counselor for help understanding the options.

How to compare Medicare plans step by step

Start with your doctors, hospitals and pharmacies. Make a list of the providers you want to keep and confirm network status directly with the plan and provider. Provider directories can be outdated, so a phone confirmation is useful. Then list every prescription, including dosage and frequency, and compare coverage in each plan's formulary. Next, estimate how often you use care. Consider regular specialist visits, imaging, outpatient procedures, hospital stays and chronic condition management. Compare premiums, deductibles, copays, coinsurance and the maximum out-of-pocket limit. Think about travel: Original Medicare may provide broader nationwide provider access, while Medicare Advantage networks can be local or regional and emergency coverage rules differ. Read the plan's Summary of Benefits, Evidence of Coverage and Annual Notice of Change. Ask whether prior authorization applies to important services and whether referrals are needed. If you speak with a broker or agent, ask how they are compensated and whether they represent all plans in your area. You can also get unbiased assistance through a State Health Insurance Assistance Program.

Avoiding Medicare scams and misleading marketing

Medicare beneficiaries are frequent targets for impersonation calls, fake cards, misleading plan advertisements and requests for personal information. Treat unsolicited calls asking for a Medicare number, bank details or immediate payment with caution. Do not share one-time security codes or click links in unexpected messages. If a caller claims that Medicare will cancel coverage unless you act immediately, hang up and contact Medicare through the official number or website. A legitimate plan representative should identify the plan and explain its coverage, costs and enrollment rules. Be cautious about claims that a plan is completely free, covers every doctor or guarantees a particular benefit without checking eligibility and service-area rules. Keep a copy of enrollment materials and confirmation numbers. If you believe you were enrolled without permission or misled, contact Medicare and your state insurance assistance office promptly. Never post your Medicare number or health information publicly. Use secure official portals and keep documents in a safe place. Report suspected fraud to the appropriate government agency. Quick reporting can help protect both your benefits and other beneficiaries.

Where to get help and verify 2026 details

Medicare.gov is the official starting point for plan comparisons, coverage rules, enrollment periods and current cost information. The Medicare Plan Finder can compare plans available by ZIP code and prescription list. CMS and Medicare.gov publish annual updates, so verify all dollar amounts and deadlines for the year you are enrolling. The State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) provides free, local counseling to people with Medicare. SHIP counselors can help compare plans and explain enrollment choices, but they do not sell insurance. Social Security handles many enrollment transactions for people turning 65, while employers and state agencies may need to provide supporting coverage information. This article is a general educational guide for US readers, not medical, legal, insurance or tax advice. Medicare rules are complex and can change. Confirm your own eligibility, premiums, enrollment rights and plan coverage directly with Medicare, Social Security, your state SHIP or the plan before making a decision.
