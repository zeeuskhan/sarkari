How US Newsrooms Decide When to Call Midterm Election Races

News organizations use official vote counts, reporting teams and projection methods to determine when a candidate can no longer catch up. A call is not a certified result.

Published November 1, 2026 · Rajasthan News 24 Desk · Updated as verified information develops

November 1, 2026: As Americans prepare to vote in the November 3 midterm elections, news organizations are explaining how they decide when a race can be projected for a candidate. The process combines vote totals, information from local election offices, reporting from the field and statistical analysis.

Reuters reported that the Associated Press plans to deploy more than 4,000 journalists and election staff for its coverage. AP's stated approach is to call a race only when the trailing candidate no longer has a realistic path to overtake the leader based on the remaining votes.

Different organizations use different methods

Decision Desk HQ gathers official results from states and combines them with reporting and statistical models. Some television networks use AP calls alongside their own analysis, exit-poll information and early-vote data. Other organizations conduct independent reviews of competitive contests.

A projection is not an official certification

Election-night projections are journalistic assessments, not the legal certification of an election. A race may remain uncalled while ballots are counted, and projections can sometimes be withdrawn if later information changes the assessment. State and local election authorities remain responsible for official results and certification.

Where to check official results

Voters should use their state election office for official vote totals, ballot-counting updates and certification information. Newsroom projections can help explain the emerging picture, but they do not replace the formal election process.
