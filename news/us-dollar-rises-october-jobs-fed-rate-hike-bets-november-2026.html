U.S. Dollar Jumps After October Jobs Beat, Raising Fed Rate-Hike Bets

Published November 6, 2026 · 2:33 p.m. ET · Rajasthan News 24 US Desk

The U.S. dollar strengthened after October payrolls exceeded expectations, while Treasury yields moved higher and traders raised the market-implied chance of a December Federal Reserve rate increase.

NEW YORK, November 6, 2026: The U.S. dollar strengthened after October payrolls exceeded expectations, while Treasury yields moved higher and traders raised the market-implied chance of a December Federal Reserve rate increase.

Jobs data shifted rate expectations

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that U.S. nonfarm payrolls increased by 165,000 in October. The unemployment rate edged down to 4.1%. Economists surveyed ahead of the release had expected a much smaller gain, Reuters reported.

After the data, traders priced in nearly an 80% chance of a quarter-point rate increase at the Fed's Dec. 8–9 meeting, up from about 67% the previous day, according to Reuters. That figure reflects market pricing and can change quickly. It is not a forecast issued by the Fed, and the Federal Open Market Committee has not committed to a December move.

What a stronger dollar can mean

A stronger dollar can make some imported goods and overseas travel cheaper for Americans, all else equal, because each dollar buys more foreign currency. The effect on retail prices is not immediate or guaranteed: exchange rates are only one part of what determines the cost of goods, and companies may adjust prices at different times.

The dollar's daily move is not a direct loan-rate quote. Mortgage rates are influenced by longer-term Treasury yields and mortgage-market conditions, while credit-card and auto-loan rates depend on other benchmarks, lender pricing and individual credit profiles. Households should not assume their borrowing costs will change one-for-one with the dollar.

What to watch next

Investors will watch upcoming inflation data and Fed officials' comments before the December meeting. A single jobs report does not settle the outlook: the central bank weighs employment, prices and broader financial conditions together.
